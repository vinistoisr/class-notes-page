Three zoom levels

Improve a service by looking at it at three levels: the value chain (which stages), the process (which steps and waits), and the encounter (what the patient actually experiences).

Generic hospital trauma pathway for illustration, not taken from the case.

Most of a patient's time is spent between steps, not in them. You find that time only at the process level, and you judge whether it matters at the encounter level.
Mapping
2

Reading a process map

A swimlane map puts each role in its own row. Every arrow that crosses a lane is a handoff; every triangle is a wait. Both are where time and errors pile up.

Illustrative map, not the case's. Tap any shape. Replace it with the case's own steps in class.

The symbols and the three kinds of time

Rectangle: activity. Diamond: decision. Triangle: wait or inventory (patients in a queue are inventory). Arrow: flow of the patient, material or information. Lanes: who does it.

Value added: the patient would pay for it and it changes their condition (resuscitation, scan, operation). Necessary, not value added: required by safety or rules (handover, documentation). Waste: waiting, rework, searching, repeated questions.

Service blueprint: a map with a line of visibility. Above it is what the patient sees (front stage); below it is the support work they never see (back stage).

Hide the waits and the map looks efficient. Show them and you see where the redesign has to happen: between lanes, at the scanner and the operating room.

Find the bottleneck

Each step can serve so many patients an hour. The step with the lowest capacity is the bottleneck, and it sets the capacity of the whole process.

StepMinutes per patientParallel resourcesCapacity / hrUtilization
Example numbers. Type in the case's own figures.
The formulas

Capacity per hour = 60 ÷ minutes per patient × number of parallel resources.

Utilization = arrival rate ÷ capacity. Above 100%, the queue in front of that step grows without limit.

Little's Law: patients in the system (L) = arrival rate (λ) × average time in the system (W). It holds for any stable process. Cut W and you free beds without adding any.

Process efficiency = value-added time ÷ total time in the system.

Adding capacity anywhere except the bottleneck changes nothing. Once you fix it, the bottleneck moves to the next-lowest step.
4

Why waits explode near 100%

With random arrivals, waiting time does not rise in a straight line. It shoots up as utilization nears 100%. A second server, or one shared line, cuts it sharply.

Same model as the instructor's "Waiting Line Single Server" and "Multiple Servers" spreadsheets (M/M/1 and M/M/s). The defaults (λ = 3, μ = 4, s = 1) match the spreadsheets.

The formulas (M/M/s)

a = λ/μ, utilization ρ = λ/(sμ). P0 = 1 ÷ [Σn=0..s-1 aⁿ/n! + aˢ/(s!(1-ρ))].

Lq = P0 · aˢ · ρ ÷ (s!(1-ρ)²). L = Lq + a. Wq = Lq/λ. W = Wq + 1/μ. For one server: Lq = λ²/(μ(μ-λ)), W = 1/(μ-λ).

Assumes random (Poisson) arrivals, exponential service times, first come first served, and no limit on the line. Real trauma arrivals come in bursts, which makes waits worse than the model.

Running a trauma bay or CT scanner near 100% busy guarantees long waits. Keeping spare capacity is a deliberate choice, not waste, when waiting costs lives.
5

Line balancing

Split the tasks among stations so that no station takes longer than the cycle time and as little time as possible sits idle. The same logic applies to splitting work among a trauma team.

Tasks, times and the cycle time of 12 are from the instructor's "Line Balancing" spreadsheet. Same four priority rules.

How the heuristic works

Open station 1. List the tasks whose predecessors are done and that fit in the time left. Pick one by the priority rule. Repeat until nothing fits, then open the next station.

Minimum stations = total task time ÷ cycle time, rounded up. Efficiency = total task time ÷ (stations × cycle time). Balance delay = 1 − efficiency.

Cycle time (or takt time) = available time ÷ required output. A shorter cycle means more output and more stations.

The rule matters less than the cycle time. The longest single task sets the shortest possible cycle, so splitting that task is often the real fix.
Improving
6

Service is different: the customer is in the process

In a factory you can buffer with inventory. In a service the customer brings variability into the process, and you either accommodate it or reduce it.

Five types of customer-introduced variability: Frei, F.X. (2006), "Breaking the Trade-Off Between Efficiency and Service", HBR. Trauma examples are mine.

Four ways services differ from manufacturing
  • Simultaneous: produced and consumed at once, so no finished-goods buffer.
  • Perishable capacity: an empty trauma bay at 3 AM can't be stored for 6 PM.
  • Customer participation: the patient's condition and history are inputs.
  • Intangible: quality is partly the experience (being told what is happening), not only the outcome.
Trauma care mostly has to accommodate (you can't refuse a patient), so the levers are on the hospital's side: spare capacity, flexible staff, and standard protocols.

From map to redesign

Once the map shows the waits and the bottleneck, a small set of levers covers most redesigns. Tap a lever to see what it changes and a trauma example.

Good redesigns combine levers: segment the flow, run steps in parallel, and protect the bottleneck. Then check the result with Little's Law: shorter time in the system means fewer patients waiting, with the same beds.

Value chains and who controls them

Ponte, Gereffi and Raj-Reichert (2019) ask two questions about any value chain: who coordinates it (governance) and how a participant moves up (upgrading).

Four kinds of upgrading
  • Process: do the same thing more efficiently (a faster trauma pathway).
  • Product: offer a better or higher-value service (a Level I trauma centre).
  • Functional: take on higher-value functions (research, training other hospitals).
  • Chain: use the capability in a new chain (the trauma system design sold as a model).

Governance types from Gereffi, Humphrey and Sturgeon (2005), the framework the handbook's introduction builds on. Summary from the published literature; check against the reading.

For a hospital, the trauma "chain" runs from paramedics to rehab across separate organizations. Redesigning one link without the handoffs on either side moves the wait somewhere else.

Glossary

Bottleneck
The step with the lowest capacity. It sets the output of the whole process.
Capacity
The most a step can process per unit of time: resources × 60 ÷ minutes per unit.
Utilization (ρ)
Demand ÷ capacity. Waits grow sharply as it nears 100%.
Flow time
Total time a unit spends in the process, including waits.
Little's Law
L = λW. Units in the system = arrival rate × time in the system.
Cycle time
Time between finished units leaving a line; the longest station time.
Takt time
Available time ÷ customer demand: the pace the process must match.
Balance delay
Share of station time left idle: 1 − efficiency.
Swimlane
A row of a process map showing one role's work.
Line of visibility
The line in a service blueprint between what the customer sees and the back stage.
Value added
Work that changes the product or the customer's condition in a way they would pay for.
M/M/s
Queue with random arrivals, random service times and s servers.
Pooling
Serving several streams from one shared queue and set of servers; cuts waits at the same utilization.
GVC governance
How a global value chain is coordinated: market, modular, relational, captive, or hierarchy.
